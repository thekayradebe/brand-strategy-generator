## Section 3 — Brand Strategy Prompt

Copy this prompt into Claude. Your brand inputs will be filled in automatically. Claude will visit the brand website and competitor URLs before writing, and will also generate a visual design direction at the end — you'll need that for the next step.

```
You are a social media brand strategist, visual brand consultant, and consumer psychology expert. I need you to create a social media brand strategy for the brand described below, plus a visual design direction for a presentation deck.

Before writing anything, visit the brand's website (if provided) and each competitor URL listed. Use what you find — their messaging, tone, content approach, visual identity, and positioning — to make this strategy more accurate and differentiated.

Then produce two outputs:

---

OUTPUT 1 — BRAND STRATEGY

1. WHY — Brand Foundation
Why does this brand exist? Define the brand's core values, mission, and positioning in the market.

Answer all four positioning questions — do not skip any:
1. Who is it for, precisely? Not a broad demographic. A specific person with a specific situation, worldview, and frustration. The narrower this is, the stronger the positioning.
2. What category does it compete in? What does the customer compare it to when making a decision? Consider whether the brand can reframe the category itself — this is one of the most powerful positioning moves available.
3. What is the single most important point of difference? Not a list. One thing. The thing this brand can own that no competitor has claimed or can credibly claim.
4. Why should anyone believe it? The reason-to-believe (RTB) — what evidence, mechanism, credential, or proof makes the point of difference credible rather than just claimed?

Then write a positioning statement using this formula:
For [specific target audience] who [have this specific problem or desire], [brand name] is the [category frame] that [point of difference] because [reason to believe].

The positioning statement is internal — it is not ad copy. It is a compass for every creative decision. If a piece of content doesn't serve the stated audience or reinforce the stated difference, it shouldn't exist.

Finally, identify the competitive whitespace this brand should occupy. Look for:
- Under-served audiences: who does the category serve badly? Who feels ignored, patronised, or over-served with things they don't need?
- Overclaimed territory: what do all competitors say? ("premium", "innovative", "community") — this is territory to avoid. The space between clichés is where brands stand out.
- Rejected category assumptions: what does every brand in this space take for granted? Rejecting one assumption is often where the strongest positioning lives.

2. WHO — Target Audience
Who is this brand speaking to? Build a five-layer audience profile:

Layer 1 — Demographics (the container): Age range, location, gender if relevant, income bracket, occupation, life stage. Necessary but insufficient — do not stop here.
Layer 2 — Psychographics (the worldview): What do they believe about themselves? What do they believe about the world? What kind of person do they aspire to be? What do they read, watch, listen to? What communities do they belong to?
Layer 3 — The problem (in their words): What specific frustration, desire, or fear is this brand addressing? Not your version of their problem — their version. How do they describe it to a friend at 11pm? What language do they actually use? Where do they express it?
Layer 4 — The awareness stage (where they are now): Are they unaware they have a problem? Problem-aware but solution-unaware? Already evaluating options? This determines what kind of content reaches them and what it needs to say.
Layer 5 — The transformation (where they want to go): What does life look like on the other side of buying? Not just the functional outcome — the identity shift. Frame it as: "I want to go from feeling like [X] to feeling like someone who [Y]."

Then create a primary persona and a secondary persona. Name them. Give each a job, a daily schedule, a specific anxiety, and a specific aspiration. The name and story are not decoration — they force specificity and make every content decision testable: "would [persona name] actually care about this?"

Then provide Tone of Voice & Personality guidelines — 3–5 bullet points describing how this brand should sound, written in the language this audience actually uses. Include a "do this / not this" example for each. Reference competitor tone to show contrast.

3. WHAT — Content Themes & Formats
What content does this brand post? Provide 3–5 content themes — these are recurring topic buckets the brand rotates through to generate consistent content ideas that speak directly to the target audience. These are not awareness stages or buyer journey phases — they are subject areas the brand owns.
For each theme, provide:
- A theme name
- A one-line description
- 2 example post ideas specific to the platform and clearly differentiated from competitors

Then outline a content format rotation strategy — explain how the brand should cycle through formats (Reels, carousels, static posts, Stories, etc.) to keep the feed dynamic and algorithm-friendly.

4. WHEN — Sample Content Calendar
When does this brand post? Provide a two-week sample content calendar (Monday–Friday) in table format. Each entry should include:
- The content idea/topic
- The content pillar it falls under
- The post format (Reel, carousel, static, Story, etc.)

---

OUTPUT 2 — BRAND VISUAL IDENTITY SYSTEM

This is not a slide theme. This is the complete visual identity system for the brand — a reference document that should be consulted every time a social media graphic, story, ad, deck, or any brand asset is created. Every decision here must be directly grounded in and justified by the brand strategy in Output 1. Do not produce generic visual recommendations. The colours, typography, imagery, and layout choices should feel like a logical, inevitable extension of the brand's positioning, audience psychology, and transformation promise.

---

SECTION A — BRAND COLOUR PALETTE

Grounded in the audience's psychology and the brand's transformation promise from Output 1, define the full working colour system:

- Primary Colour: the dominant brand colour. Give the hex code, the name you'd give it (e.g., "Deep Espresso", "Soft Clay"), and a 1–2 sentence explanation of why this colour is right for this specific audience and positioning — reference the psychographics and transformation.
- Secondary Colour: a supporting colour that complements the primary. Same format.
- Accent Colour: one high-contrast colour used sparingly for calls to action, highlights, and moments of emphasis. Same format.
- Neutral(s): 1–2 background and text neutrals (near-white, off-white, cream, charcoal, black, etc.) — describe when to use each.
- What to avoid: name 1–2 colour directions this brand should never go near, and explain why they would undermine the positioning.

---

SECTION B — TYPOGRAPHY SYSTEM

Define the complete type system for all brand touchpoints — social media graphics, Stories, carousels, presentation decks, and any other assets:

- Display / Headline Font: the font used for big statements, cover slides, hero graphics, and carousel title frames. Name a specific Google Font (or font style). Describe the visual character. Explain why it fits this brand's tone and audience.
- Body / Supporting Font: the font used for body copy, supporting text, and captions in graphics. Name a specific Google Font. Describe when and how to use it.
- Accent / Detail Font: optional — a third font used sparingly for labels, callouts, or decorative text if the brand's aesthetic calls for it. If not needed, say so and explain why.
- Type Hierarchy Rules: describe how headline, subheadline, body, and label text should relate to each other in size and weight. Give a concrete example using the brand name and a sample headline.
- Type Personality: one sentence capturing the overall typographic mood (e.g., "Confident and editorial — big, heavy headlines next to clean, light body text" or "Warm and readable — rounded letterforms at every size, no harsh contrast").

---

SECTION C — IMAGERY & VISUAL CONTENT DIRECTION

What this brand looks like in every visual context — informed by the audience's aspirations, lifestyle, and the transformation they're buying into:

- Photography Style: describe the specific visual feel of photos this brand uses. Reference lighting, colour temperature, setting, subject matter, and emotional register. Be specific enough that someone could brief a photographer or find matching stock images (e.g., "Candid lifestyle shots in natural light — warm, golden-hour tones, real environments not studios, subjects mid-action rather than posed, always shot at eye-level or slightly below to feel intimate not aspirational").
- What the Subject Looks Like: describe the people, settings, or objects that should appear in imagery — tied directly to the primary persona from Output 1.
- Illustration / Graphic Style: if this brand uses flat graphics, icons, patterns, or illustrative elements — describe the style (e.g., minimal line-work, bold geometric shapes, organic hand-drawn textures). If photography-first, say so.
- Textures & Overlays: describe any background textures, grain, gradients, or overlays that are part of the aesthetic — or explicitly state "clean and flat, no texture" if that fits the brand.
- Imagery to Avoid: name 1–2 visual directions that would feel off-brand, and briefly explain why they conflict with the positioning or audience psychology.

---

SECTION D — LAYOUT & COMPOSITION PRINCIPLES

How space, structure, and visual weight are used across all brand assets:

- Spatial Style: describe the overall approach to layout (e.g., "open and breathing — lots of whitespace, single focal point per frame" or "dense and layered — multiple elements in conversation, structured grid with intentional overlap").
- Grid & Alignment: describe how elements are organised — centred, asymmetric, edge-to-edge, structured columns.
- Graphic Elements: describe any recurring design motifs — lines, frames, dividers, shapes, logo lock-ups, or brand marks — and how they're used.
- Social Media Format Notes: give brief layout guidance specific to the primary platform from the brand details (e.g., for Instagram: how to treat the top third of a carousel cover, how to design for mobile-first vertical viewing, safe zones for text on Stories).

---

SECTION E — OVERALL VISUAL IDENTITY STATEMENT

Write one paragraph (4–6 sentences) that captures the complete visual world of this brand. This should read like a creative brief — specific enough that a designer who has never met the client could open it and immediately understand the aesthetic direction. Reference the audience, the transformation, and the positioning from Output 1. End with a single "visual identity tagline" — one punchy sentence that names the aesthetic (e.g., "Premium and editorial — like a luxury magazine that actually talks to real people." or "Raw and grounded — like a fitness brand that trains in the real world, not a glossy ad.").

---

SECTION F — APPLICATION TO THE PORTFOLIO DECK

Using everything defined above, describe how this visual identity should be applied specifically to the 10-page portfolio deck that will be built in NotebookLM. Include:

- Slide background treatment (colour, texture, or gradient — reference Section A and C)
- Headline and body type treatment per slide (reference Section B)
- Imagery placement and style for slides that include visuals (reference Section C)
- Any slide-specific layout notes for the cover, content theme slides, and the calendar slide

---

Brand Details:
- Business Name: [business name]
- Website: [business website URL or "none provided"]
- Industry: [industry / niche]
- Offer: [what they sell]
- Target Audience: [who they help]
- Before (The Problem): [before — the problem]
- After (The Transformation): [after — the transformation]
- Tone: [tone of voice]
- Competitor URLs: [competitor URL 1], [competitor URL 2], [competitor URL 3]
- Platform: [primary platform]

Write in a professional but approachable tone. Keep the strategy practical and actionable. Keep the visual identity system specific and opinionated — every recommendation should be grounded in the brand strategy, the audience psychology, and the transformation promise. Never give vague or generic visual options. If you can't justify a visual choice by referencing something in the brand strategy, it doesn't belong here.
```

## Section 4 — Build Your Portfolio Deck in NotebookLM

Once Claude has generated your brand strategy and brand visual identity system, you'll save each output as its own Google Doc before opening NotebookLM. Keeping them separate makes NotebookLM much more accurate — it can draw on the strategy and the visual identity independently, and knows exactly what each document contains.

### Step 1 — Save Your Claude Outputs as Google Docs

**Doc 1 — Brand Strategy**
1. Copy everything under **OUTPUT 1 — BRAND STRATEGY** from your Claude conversation.
2. Go to [docs.google.com](https://docs.google.com) and create a new document.
3. Paste the text and name the document: `Brand Strategy — [BrandName]`
4. Leave the document open — you'll upload it to NotebookLM in a moment.

**Doc 2 — Brand Visual Identity System**
1. Copy everything under **OUTPUT 2 — BRAND VISUAL IDENTITY SYSTEM** from your Claude conversation.
2. Create a second new Google Doc.
3. Paste the text and name the document: `Brand Visual Identity — [BrandName]`
4. Leave this document open as well.

> You'll reuse this visual identity document in later building blocks whenever you're creating graphics, ads, or any brand asset. Keep it somewhere you can find it easily.

---

### Step 2 — Set Up Your NotebookLM Notebook

1. Go to [notebooklm.google.com](https://notebooklm.google.com) and create a new notebook.
2. Click **Add source** and upload your first Google Doc: `Brand Strategy — [BrandName]`
3. Click **Add source** again and upload your second Google Doc: `Brand Visual Identity — [BrandName]`
4. NotebookLM now has both documents as separate, labelled sources. When you send the prompt below, it will draw from both automatically.

---

### Step 3 — Generate Your Deck

Paste the prompt below into the NotebookLM chat. You don't need to paste any strategy or design content — it's already in your sources.

```
Using the brand strategy and brand visual identity system I've provided as your sources, create a structured 10-page presentation deck for my portfolio. The visual identity document contains a complete colour palette, typography system, imagery direction, layout principles, and a deck-specific application guide in Section F — use all of it. Every slide should feel like a cohesive expression of the brand, not a generic template.

Page 1 — Cover
Headline: brand name + tagline
Subheading: "Social Media Brand Strategy by [Your Name]"
Design note: apply the primary background colour and display font from the visual identity. Brand name in the accent colour. Bold, minimal, and immediately on-brand.

Page 2 — Brand Foundation (WHY)
Content: the four positioning questions answered, the positioning statement, and the competitive whitespace
Design note: lead with the positioning statement as the dominant typographic element. Supporting content (positioning questions, whitespace) in a structured two-column layout using the body font and secondary colour.

Page 3 — Target Audience (WHO)
Content: the five-layer audience profile — lead with psychographics and the transformation, supported by demographics, awareness stage, and the primary persona
Design note: open with a large typographic pull-quote using the persona's voice (their version of the problem, Layer 3). Remaining layers as tight supporting copy below. Persona name and descriptor as a styled label using the accent colour.

Page 4 — Tone & Voice
Content: the tone of voice guidelines — do/don't pairs as a clean list
Design note: each guideline as its own row. Accent colour for the "do" side, a muted neutral for the "don't" side. Typography should itself demonstrate the brand's tone — if the brand is bold, make this slide bold.

Page 5 — Content Theme 1
Content: theme name, one-line description, 2 example post ideas
Design note: theme name as a large display headline in the primary or accent colour. Examples as indented supporting copy. Background from the visual identity's slide treatment.

Page 6 — Content Theme 2
Same structure as Page 5

Page 7 — Content Theme 3 (expand to Pages 8 and 9 if the strategy includes 4–5 themes, one theme per slide)
Same structure as Page 5

Page 8 — Content Formats & Rotation (WHAT continued)
Content: the format rotation strategy — how the brand cycles through Reels, carousels, static posts, Stories, etc.
Design note: each format as its own row with a short note on when and why to use it. Use the accent colour for format labels. Layout should feel like a quick-reference guide — structured and scannable.

Page 9 — Sample Content Calendar (WHEN)
Content: Extract the two-week Monday–Friday content calendar directly from the source material and display it as a structured table with four columns: Day, Content Idea, Content Theme, and Format. Pull all row data exactly as they appear in the source — do not invent or infer any values.
Design note: alternate row shading using the brand's neutral and secondary colours. Theme names as colour-coded pill labels matching the theme slides. Table is the dominant element on the slide with a short branded header above it.

Page 10 — Next Steps
Content: the top 3 actions the social media manager should take first to bring this strategy to life
Design note: numbered list, each item opens with a bold action verb in the accent colour. Closing line at the bottom: "Built with AI. Designed for results."

For each page, write the full slide content AND describe exactly what the background, layout, typography, and visual elements should look like — referencing specific colours, fonts, and layout rules from the visual identity document. The visual identity is your style guide. Apply it consistently across all 10 pages.
```

## Section 5 — Refine Your Deck

Once NotebookLM has generated your deck, you have two options for refining it — depending on whether you have a Canva Pro account.

---

### Option A — Refine Directly in NotebookLM *(no paid tools required)*

If you don't have Canva Pro, you can make changes without leaving NotebookLM.

**To edit text or restructure a slide:**
- Go back into the NotebookLM chat and ask it to revise specific pages. For example: *"Rewrite Page 5 — the content pillar description is too long. Make it shorter and punchier."*
- You can ask it to regenerate individual pages without redoing the whole deck.

**To add images:**
- In NotebookLM, you can add images directly to your notebook sources or into the generated output.
- Find the image you want to use (a product photo, a brand asset, a screenshot from the client's website) and upload it into NotebookLM as an additional source.
- Then prompt NotebookLM: *"Using the image I've added, update Page [X] to include this image as the main visual on that slide."*

This approach is more manual and takes longer, but it works without any paid tools.

---

### Option B — Refine in Canva Pro with Magic Layers *(recommended)*

> **Note:** Magic Layers is a **Canva Pro feature** — it is not available on the free plan. As a social media manager, a Canva Pro account is a core professional tool you'll use constantly. If you don't have it yet, this is a good time to get it.

Magic Layers lets you take a finished slide image, drop it into Canva, and have Canva automatically separate it into individual editable elements — text, background, shapes, images — so you can edit each one independently. This is the fastest way to customise the NotebookLM deck with real client assets.

**What you need:**
- Canva Pro account
- Your 10-page deck exported from NotebookLM as individual images (one PNG per slide)
- Optional: screenshots of product or service images from the client's website

---

**Step 1 — Export Your Slides from NotebookLM**

Download or screenshot each slide individually from NotebookLM.
Save them as: `slide_01.png`, `slide_02.png` ... `slide_10.png`

---

**Step 2 — Set Up Your Canva Presentation**

1. Go to [canva.com](https://canva.com) and click **Create a design**.
2. Search for **Presentation** and select the **1920 × 1080px** (widescreen) format.
3. You'll have a blank deck — add 10 slides, one for each exported image.

---

**Step 3 — Import Slides and Separate Layers with Magic Layers**

For each slide:
1. Upload the slide PNG via **Uploads → Upload files**.
2. Drag it onto the slide canvas and resize it to fill the full frame.
3. Select the image, then click **Edit Image → Magic Layers** in the toolbar.
4. Canva will analyse the image and separate it into individual editable layers — text blocks, shapes, background, photos — so you can click and edit each element independently.
5. Click any text layer to rewrite it. Click any image layer to swap or delete it.

> Magic Layers works best on slides with clearly defined elements (text on a background, an image next to copy). If a slide is very dense, work through the simpler elements first.

---

**Step 4 — Add Client Product or Service Images**

If you want to replace a placeholder visual with a real client photo:

1. Go to the client's website and take a screenshot of the product or service image you want to use. If possible, right-click the image and select **Save image as** for a higher resolution file.
2. In Canva, upload the image via **Uploads → Upload files**.
3. Click the uploaded image, then go to **Edit Image → Background Remover**. Canva will cut out the background and isolate the product.
4. Drag the cleaned image onto the relevant slide and position it to replace the placeholder.

---

**Step 5 — Final Polish and Export**

1. Review all 10 slides. Check that text is readable, images are placed correctly, and the visual theme is consistent across every page.
2. When you're happy, click **Share → Download**.
3. Choose **PDF Print** for a high-resolution single file, or **PNG** if you want individual slide images.
